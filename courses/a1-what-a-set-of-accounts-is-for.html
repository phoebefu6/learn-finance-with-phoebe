What a set of accounts is for

In month 10, Marchmont Instruments earned £52,781. It was the best month in the company's history, every month before it had also been profitable, and the finance director had nothing to apologise for. In the same month the bank balance went to minus £7,432. Both numbers are correct, both come out of the same set of books, and they are not in conflict. They are answers to two different questions, and most people who are handed accounts have never been told that more than one question is being asked. Tonight is about what the three statements are each for, and why the one that would have warned you is the one nobody reads.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The three questions a set of accounts answers, and the ability to say which statement to open for which question rather than reaching for the profit line every time. LiveWhy three statements and not one4 min▶

A single number cannot describe a business, because a business raises three questions that have different shapes. Did we make money over this period? What do we own and owe right now? And where did the actual money go? The first two are not the same question, the third is not implied by either, and no one of them can be derived from the other two on its own.

So the accounts are three documents, not one, and they are designed to be read together. The habit worth building is not memorising line items. It is knowing which document to open, which is a decision you can make in about two seconds once you know that there are three.

Real world

Marchmont's month 10: the income statement says £52,781 of profit, the best month the company has had. The balance sheet says the cash line is minus £7,432. The cash flow statement says why, and it is the only one of the three that answers the question the finance director was actually being asked, which was whether payroll would clear.

Self-studyThe words that will keep coming back4 min▶
  • Income statement. Also called the P&L. Covers a period and answers whether the business made money over it. Session 2, and practitioner session 2.
  • Balance sheet. A single moment, not a period. What the business owns and what it owes on one specific day. Practitioner session 5.
  • Cash flow statement. Covers a period and answers where the money physically moved. Session 3, and practitioner session 4.
  • Stock and flow. A stock is a level at a moment, like a bank balance. A flow is a movement over a period, like revenue. Confusing the two is the single most common misreading of accounts.
  • Accrual. Recording revenue when it is earned rather than when it is collected. The reason profit and cash separate at all. Session 2.
  • Working capital. The money tied up in unpaid customer invoices and unsold stock, less what the business has not yet paid its own suppliers. Practitioner session 3.
  • Valuation. What a business is worth, which is a different subject with a different course. Not covered here.
Part 1 · covers the three questions

Three questions, and the statement that answers each

10 min live

Each statement has a shape. Two of them cover a stretch of time and one of them is a photograph of a single day. Knowing which is which stops the most common mistake in the room, which is answering a cash question with a profit number.

LiveA stock is not a flow4 min▶

Revenue, profit and cash generated are flows: they only mean something attached to a period. Cash in the bank, debt outstanding and stock on the shelf are levels: they only mean something attached to a date. A flow with no period and a level with no date are both unreadable, which is why "we made £52,781" and "we have £52,781" are completely different statements and are constantly confused in conversation.

The practical consequence is that a healthy flow and a dangerous level can coexist, which is exactly Marchmont's month 10. The profit flow was the strongest it had ever been. The cash level had been falling every month for nine months and finally went through zero. Asking "are we profitable" would have got a reassuring answer to a question nobody needed answered.

LiveWhich statement to open3 min▶

Three questions come up constantly in a review, and each has a home. Whether a product line earns its keep is an income statement question. Whether the business can survive the next quarter is a cash flow statement question. Whether it could borrow, and against what, is a balance sheet question.

Almost all of the confusion in finance conversations with non-finance people comes from a cash question being answered with a profit number, because the profit number is the one that gets circulated. It is not a trick: the profit number is genuinely the headline, it is just the headline for a different question.

Self-studyWhy the balance sheet has to balance4 min▶

Everything a business owns was funded by somebody: a lender, an owner, or profits it kept. So what it owns must equal what it owes plus what the owners have in it. That is not a convention chosen for tidiness, it is an accounting identity, and it holds at every moment or the books are wrong.

This makes the balance sheet a genuine error check rather than a report. A model of a business whose balance sheet does not balance has a mistake in it somewhere, and the failure to balance is how you find the mistake. Practitioner session 5 builds the check and then deliberately breaks it, because watching it fail is the fastest way to understand what it is testing.

Part 2 · covers the month 10 pair

The best month, and the month the money ran out

7 min live

Marchmont's first ten months make the point better than any definition. One column goes up every single month. The other goes down every single month. They are computed from the same transactions.

LiveWhere the difference goes4 min▶

Over those first twelve months Marchmont earned £445,631 of net income and generated minus £297,261 of free cash flow. The gap is £742,892, and it is not a rounding difference or a fraud. Of it, £664,607 is money sitting in unpaid customer invoices and unsold stock.

Marchmont sells to laboratories that pay on 75 day terms, and it holds two months of finished instruments so it can ship on request. Every extra pound of sales therefore arrives as a promise first and as money much later, while the wages and the materials that produced it are paid now. Growth makes this worse rather than better, which is session 4 and is the part that catches people out.

LiveThe question worth asking in the meeting3 min▶

When somebody presents a profit figure, one question does most of the work: and what happened to cash over the same period? It is not a hostile question and it does not require any finance background to ask. If the two numbers move together, there is nothing to discuss. If they diverge, the reason is always interesting and is usually about timing rather than performance.

At Marchmont the honest answer in month 10 would have been that profit was the best it had ever been and cash was negative, because the business was funding its own growth out of its bank balance. That is a fundable situation and a survivable one. It is only dangerous when nobody says it out loud until the balance is gone.

Self-studyWhat this is not3 min▶

A profit and cash gap is not automatically a warning sign. A business that is growing and sells on credit will always show one, and the absence of a gap in a fast growing company would itself be odd. What matters is whether the gap is understood, sized and funded, which is session 6.

Nor is it a valuation question. What Marchmont is worth, and whether anybody should buy it, uses different tools and is a different course. Nothing here is a view about whether any business is a good investment.

Part 3 · build-along

Read one month of Marchmont

20 min hands on

No spreadsheet. Six steps, done out loud together, on the month 10 figures. The aim is to route each question to the right statement without looking anything up.

Say what month 10 was, in one sentence each way

Write the profit sentence and the cash sentence side by side: "Marchmont earned £52,781 in month 10" and "Marchmont ended month 10 with minus £7,432 in the bank". Notice that neither sentence contradicts the other and that they would lead to opposite decisions.

Route three questions

For each of these, name the statement: can we pay the suppliers next week, is the instrument line making money, and could we borrow against what we own. If any of the three took more than a few seconds, that is the one to remember.

Find the flow that is a level in disguise

Of revenue, cash at bank, net income, stock on hand and free cash flow, pick out the two that are levels. Then say what date they belong to, because a level with no date is not an answer.

Put the £742,892 somewhere

Twelve month profit was £445,631 and free cash flow was minus £297,261. Say where the difference physically is. It is not missing and it is not spent: name the two places it is sitting.

Ask the meeting question on a real number

Take any profit figure from your own organisation and ask what happened to cash over the same period. Write down the answer you get, or write down that nobody had it to hand, which is itself the finding.

Decide what you would have wanted in month 6

In month 6 Marchmont still had £95,015 in the bank and was earning £33,908 a month. Say what you would have wanted on the page in front of you at that point, and who should have been the one to bring it.

The habit to keep. Two numbers travel together or neither of them means much: the profit for the period, and the change in cash over the same period. Presenting one without the other is not dishonest, it is just incomplete, and the incompleteness is always in the same direction because profit is the flattering one.

Check yourself

Three questions

Marchmont's month 10 shows net income of £52,781 and cash of minus £7,432. What has gone wrong?

A · Someone has made an error, because a profitable month cannot end with less cash
B · Nothing has gone wrong in the books; profit is a flow over the month and cash is a level at the end of it, and the money earned is sitting in unpaid invoices and stock
C · The company is making a loss that the income statement is hiding
The two figures answer different questions and are computed from the same transactions. Over twelve months the gap is £742,892, of which £664,607 is the build in unpaid customer invoices and unsold stock. That is a timing difference, not an error and not a hidden loss.

A colleague asks whether the business can pay its suppliers next week. Which statement answers that?

A · The income statement, because it shows whether the business is profitable enough to afford them
B · Any of them, since all three are computed from the same data
C · The cash flow statement, supported by the cash line on the balance sheet, because the question is about money moving and money held
Profitability does not pay a supplier; cleared funds do. Marchmont was profitable in every one of its first ten months and still hit minus £7,432. The income statement would have answered a question nobody asked.

Why must the balance sheet balance?

A · Because everything the business owns was funded by a lender, an owner, or retained profit, so what it owns equals what it owes plus what the owners have in it
B · Because accountants agreed on the convention to make the documents easier to compare
C · Because the income statement and the cash flow statement are always equal to each other
It is an identity, not a convention, which is what makes it useful as an error check: a set of books that does not balance has a mistake in it and the imbalance is how you find it.
Before session 2

Homework

Twenty minutes, on your own organisation or any company whose accounts are public.

Find all three

Locate the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement in one set of published accounts. Note how far apart they are in the document and which one is quoted in the summary at the front.

Compare two numbers

Write down profit for the period and the change in cash over the same period. If they differ by more than a little, write down one sentence about where you think the difference went. Session 3 checks your answer.
