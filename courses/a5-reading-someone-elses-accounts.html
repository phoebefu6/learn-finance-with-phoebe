Learn Finance with Phoebe · Leader session 5 of 6

Reading someone else's accounts

Sessions 1 to 4 read a set of books from the inside, where every assumption was visible. Tonight somebody hands you a document instead: a supplier you are about to depend on, a partner asking for terms, a company you are being asked to merge with, an employer. The figures in it were prepared to a standard and checked to a standard, and neither of those standards was designed to answer your question. That is not a scandal and it is not a reason to distrust the document. It is a reason to know what the document is for, where inside it the information actually sits, and what it is allowed to leave out.

Part 0

A document built for a different reader

A set of published accounts is written for everyone and therefore for nobody in particular. It has to serve a lender, a supplier, a tax authority, a shareholder and a competitor at once, which means it is general by design. Your question is specific: can this company pay me, will it still be here next year, is the business it describes the business it claims to be. Tonight is the gap between those two things, and how to close as much of it as the document allows.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear line between what preparation to a standard gives you and what an audit gives you, so you stop expecting either to certify the number you care about. The habit of going to the notes before reacting to the face of the statements. And an order to work in when a set of accounts lands on your desk, cheapest checks first, so the first five minutes are spent well.

LiveUseful, and not omniscient4 min▶

A set of accounts is the most reliable description of a company available to somebody who is not inside it. Somebody spent months assembling it, somebody independent tested it, and it follows a shape you can learn once and then read anywhere. That is a great deal more than you get about most things you have to make decisions about.

What it is not is a complete account of the business. It reports a period that has closed and a day that has passed, at a level of detail chosen for a general reader, using measurement choices made by the people who prepared it. Reading it well means keeping both halves of that in mind: taking it seriously, and knowing precisely which of your questions it was never built to answer.

Real world

Marchmont's first twelve months, published as one annual set, would show £445,631 of profit and a company that traded profitably in a growing market. Nothing in that document is wrong. It would also not show month 10, when the bank balance went below zero, and it would not show that the deepest point, -£115,501 in month 19, was still ahead.

Self-studyThe parts of the document3 min▶
  • The face of the statements. The three statements from session 1, each a page or two of totals. This is what gets quoted and it is the smallest part of the document.
  • The notes. Everything behind those totals: the measurement choices, the breakdowns, what is committed but not yet spent, what happened after the period ended. Usually the bulk of the pages.
  • The auditor's report. A short statement of what was checked, to what standard, and with what conclusion. Worth reading in full because it is short and because the exceptions live in it.
  • The narrative at the front. Written by the company about itself. Useful for understanding what management thinks matters, and not a source of verified numbers.
  • Comparatives. The previous period printed alongside the current one, which is where most of the information in a single set of accounts actually comes from.
Part 1 · covers the two promises

What the accounts promise, and what they do not

13 min live

Two separate assurances are attached to a published set, and they are routinely confused with each other and with a third thing that neither of them offers.

LivePrepared to a standard4 min▶

A standard settles how things are measured and how they are presented, so that the revenue line in one company means roughly what the revenue line in another company means, and so that this year sits next to last year honestly. That consistency is the whole value: without it, comparison is impossible and every set of accounts would need its own translator.

What a standard does not do is remove judgement. It narrows the range of defensible answers and then leaves the choice inside that range to the people who know the business, which is the only place the choice can sensibly sit. Two well run companies in the same trade can therefore report different profit on identical activity, with nobody at fault.

LiveWhat the audit opinion covers4 min▶

An audit is an independent test of whether the statements, taken as a whole, are free from error large enough to change the conclusions of a reasonable reader. That threshold is the crux. Something can be too small to matter to the accounts as a whole and be precisely the thing your decision turns on: a single customer balance, one contract, the line you happen to be exposed to.

An opinion is also a conclusion about the statements, not a verdict on the business. A company can be audited, clean, and in serious difficulty, and a company can be unaudited and perfectly healthy. Read the auditor's report anyway, in full: it is short, it says what the standard was, and anything the auditor felt the need to draw attention to is in there.

Self-studyThree things people expect and do not get3 min▶
  • A statement that the business will survive. The accounts describe a period that has ended. Survival is a forecast, and forecasts are not in the document except where the preparers chose to discuss them.
  • A guarantee that every figure is right. The test is applied to the whole. Small things are not individually certified, and small to the accounts may be large to you.
  • Enough detail to answer a specific question. A single line can contain the answer and hide it. The notes are where the line is broken apart, and if the breakdown you need is not there, the document does not have it.
Part 2 · covers where the information is

The notes are the document

14 min live

The three statements are a few pages of totals. Everything that makes those totals mean anything is behind them, in the part most readers skip, and one more layer out there is a set of things that are simply not in the document at all.

LiveWhat the notes are for4 min▶

A single line on the face of a statement is an aggregate, and an aggregate can be built in several defensible ways. The notes say which way was chosen and what went into it. They are where a revenue figure becomes revenue by type, where a receivables figure becomes receivables by age, and where a cost line splits into the parts that recur and the parts that will not.

They also carry things that have no home on the face at all: money the company has committed to spend but has not spent, obligations it has guaranteed for someone else, dealings with parties it is connected to, and events after the period ended that a reader ought to know about. None of that changes a total. All of it changes what the totals mean.

LiveThe judgement calls, and where they hide4 min▶

Session 2 made the point that profit is an opinion. In your own accounts you can see the opinion being formed. In somebody else's, the same opinions have already been formed and then compressed into a total, and the only trace is a paragraph in the policies note.

The recurring ones are worth knowing by name. When revenue is recognised, which decides whether a contract lands in this period or the next. What is treated as a cost now and what is treated as an asset to be spread over later years. How long an asset is spread over: Marchmont writes its equipment off over 60 months, and a company spreading identical equipment over a longer life reports more profit each year with no difference whatever in the cash. How much of what customers owe is judged uncollectable. What unsold stock is judged to be worth. Each of these is a legitimate choice inside the standard, each moves profit, and none of them is visible unless you go and look.

LiveWhere practitioners genuinely disagree4 min▶

How much judgement should a standard leave in the hands of preparers? This is an old argument between two coherent positions, and it has not been settled.

One position: narrow it. Judgement is where comparability dies and where pressure gets applied, and a reader outside the company cannot audit a state of mind. A tighter rule produces numbers that may fit some businesses badly but that mean the same thing everywhere, which is what an outside reader needs most.

The other position: keep it. A rule tight enough to remove judgement will describe some businesses wrongly, and a wrong number that is comparable is worse than a right number that needs a note. Management knows which contracts are real and which stock will sell, and a standard that refuses to use that knowledge throws away the best information in the building.

Both sides are right about what the other side costs. The practical consequence for a reader is the same either way: the policies note is not boilerplate, it is the terms on which every figure in the document was produced.

Self-studyHow old it is, and how little you may get4 min▶

Age. The balance sheet describes one specific day, and that day is behind you before the document exists. The books have to be closed, the figures assembled, an audit performed where there is one, and the document approved and filed. By the time a copy reaches you the gap is measured in months, and the company has been trading for all of them. Nothing in the document tells you what it did with that time.

Size. How much you are given depends on how large the company is. Above a size threshold you can expect a full set with extensive notes and an audit. Below it, in many places, a company may file a reduced set: fewer notes, less breakdown, sometimes no cash flow statement and sometimes no audit at all. The thresholds and the exact reliefs differ by jurisdiction and change over time, so check what applies where the company is registered rather than assuming. The practical point is that a small supplier may legitimately give you two of the three questions from session 1 and nothing behind them, and that the missing statement is most often the one on cash.

Part 3 · build-along

The first five minutes with an unfamiliar set

15 min hands on

In this order, cheapest first. Each step costs less than the one after it, and any of them can end the exercise by telling you the document cannot answer your question. Use a real set: a supplier's, a customer's, or any company whose accounts are public.

Check what you are actually holding

Which legal entity, which period, how many months it covers, and whether it has been audited. Seconds, and it decides how much everything after it is worth. A group and one company inside that group are different documents about different things.

See whether there is a cash flow statement

If there is not, you have two of the three questions from session 1 and you will not get the third from this document. That is not necessarily concealment, it is often what a smaller company is required to file, but it changes what you can conclude and it is worth knowing in the first minute rather than the fortieth.

Put profit and the change in cash side by side

The same pairing as session 1, for the same period. If they move together there is little to chase. If they diverge, the reason is in the working capital lines, and session 3 taught you how to read them. Marchmont's first year would pair £445,631 of profit with -£297,261 of free cash flow.

Read the accounting policies note before reacting to any number

Five minutes, usually near the front of the notes. Revenue recognition, asset lives, provisions against what customers owe, how stock is valued. You are not auditing the choices, you are finding out what the numbers on the face actually mean.

Read last year's column, then look for restatements

One set of accounts is a snapshot; two periods side by side is the cheapest trend you will ever get, and it is printed in the same document. Then check whether any prior figure was restated and why, because a changed comparative is a note worth reading.

Write down the questions the document cannot answer

What the cash balance is today, what the deepest point in the current plan is, what happened in the months since the period ended, whether the one customer you care about pays on time. These need a person. Arriving at that conversation with a specific list, rather than a general unease, is the whole return on the previous five steps.

The habit to keep. Read the notes first and the totals second. The totals are what everyone remembers and the notes are what everyone skips, so the notes are where the information that other people in the room do not have is sitting, in plain sight, already written down for you.

Check yourself

Three questions

A set of accounts carries a clean audit opinion. What have you been told?

A · That every figure in the document has been individually verified as correct
B · That in the auditor's opinion the statements as a whole are free from error large enough to change a reasonable reader's conclusions, which is not a verdict on the business and not a certificate on any single line
C · That the company is financially sound and can be relied on to meet its obligations
The test is applied to the statements as a whole. A balance too small to matter at that level can be the exact balance your exposure sits in, and a business can be audited, clean and in difficulty at the same time.

Two companies in the same trade report different profit on what looks like identical activity. Where do you look first?

A · Nowhere; one of them must have made an error, and the accounts cannot both be right
B · The narrative at the front, where management explains the performance
C · The accounting policies note in each set, because a standard narrows the range of defensible measurement choices without removing them
Revenue timing, asset lives, provisions and stock valuation are all judgements made inside the standard. Marchmont writes equipment off over 60 months; a company using a longer life reports more profit each year on identical equipment and identical cash.

You need to know whether a supplier can pay its people next month. What can the published accounts give you?

A · The position on a day that has already passed, plus whatever the notes say about events after it, which is useful background and not an answer to the question asked
B · A direct answer, since the cash line on the balance sheet shows what is in the bank
C · Nothing of any value, because published accounts are always too old to be worth reading
The cash line is a level on one specific day, months ago. Marchmont's own figures make the point: the month 12 position of -£47,261 says nothing about the deepest point of -£115,501 still ahead in month 19.
Before session 6

Homework

Twenty five minutes with one real set of accounts belonging to a company you are not part of.

Run the six steps and time yourself

Work in order and note where you stopped, or where the document stopped you. If there was no cash flow statement, write down what you could and could not conclude without it.

Bring back three questions and one judgement

Three questions the document cannot answer and that you would put to a person, and one measurement choice from the policies note that materially affects what the profit figure means. Session 6 is about taking a number into a room and defending it, and these are the questions that will be put to you.

Cheat sheet

Two promises. Prepared to a standard means comparable. Audited to a standard means an opinion on the whole. Neither is an answer to your question.
Materiality. Measured against the accounts as a whole. Small to them can be large to you.
The notes are the document. The face is totals; the notes are what the totals mean.
The judgements to look for. Revenue timing, asset lives, provisions against what customers owe, stock valuation.
Age. One day that has already passed, reaching you months later, with the company trading throughout.
Size. Smaller companies can often file less, and the cash flow statement is the piece most often missing.
The order. What am I holding, is there a cash flow statement, profit against cash, policies note, last year's column, then the questions for a person.
The posture. Useful, not omniscient. Take the document seriously and know exactly what it was never built to tell you.
